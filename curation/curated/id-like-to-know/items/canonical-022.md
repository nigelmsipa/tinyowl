## Entry `canonical-022a`

- `question`: `If Adam and Eve were perfectly created in a perfect garden, why would God ask them to tend and keep it? How does one maintain something already perfect?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 2:15; Genesis 3:19; Revelation 16:15`

Revelation 16:15 cited by Murray as an analogy about keeping white robes clean. Genesis 3 referenced generally regarding thorns and thistles but not formally cited by verse.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White, The Story of Redemption, page 21; Ellen G. White, Our High Calling, page 223`
- `tags`: `Genesis-2, garden-of-Eden, labor, occupation, idleness, perfect-creation, pruning, Ellen-White, pre-sin-work`

### Answer

Perfection does not mean maintenance-free. The garden of Eden required tending — pruning branches, planting, care — but the work was pleasant and invigorating, not burdensome.

This labor was God's gift to Adam for three reasons. First, it gave him purpose and occupation. Murray notes that every creature of God has a purpose; idleness is not a blessing. Second, it kept him engaged and delighted. Ellen White (Story of Redemption, page 21) writes: "A portion of their time was to be occupied in the happy employment of dressing the garden. Their labor was not wearisome but pleasant and invigorating." Third, employment was how Adam could exercise his capacities and find happiness — to "call into exercise the wonderful organs of the body" (Our High Calling, page 223).

The key distinction is between pre-sin and post-sin labor. Genesis 3:19 establishes the post-Fall curse: "in the sweat of thy face shall you eat bread." Before sin, the same work — pruning, beautifying, maintaining — was pleasant. After sin it became toilsome. Eden's labor was intentional stewardship of what was perfect, keeping it in pristine condition.

Even in the eternal ages to come, occupations continue. As Revelation 16:15 says we must keep our garments clean, so Adam and Eve kept the garden — perfect, if you please. Maintaining perfection is an active responsibility, not an oxymoron.

## Entry `canonical-022b`

- `question`: `Why do Adventists teach that the little horn of Daniel 8 is not Antiochus Epiphanes, especially given that Jesus himself attended the feast of dedication (John 10:22) commemorating the Jews' deliverance from Antiochus and restoration of the sanctuary?`

- `secondary_support`: ``
- `tags`: `Daniel-8, Daniel-7, little-horn, Antiochus-Epiphanes, sanctuary-cleansing, year-day-principle, heavenly-sanctuary, Rome-papal, 2300-days, prophecy, time-of-the-end`

### Answer

The identification of Daniel 8's little horn as Antiochus Epiphanes fails on theological, chronological, and exegetical grounds.

The little horn of Daniel 8 is the same little horn of Daniel 7 — not a horn emerging from one of the four Greek kingdoms themselves. Bohr argues that the traditional identification of the four horns as Alexander's four generals is incorrect: one of those four horns is actually Pergamum, located in Asia Minor, which was the historical origin of pagan Rome and later papal Rome. The little horn of Daniel 8 therefore represents Roman power growing out of Pergamum, not a Seleucid king.

The sanctuary in Daniel 8:14 to be "cleansed" cannot be the earthly temple in Jerusalem. That temple was destroyed in AD 70. By the end of the 2300-day prophecy no physical sanctuary would exist to cleanse. The sanctuary in view is the heavenly one, which operates during the Christian dispensation. Jesus's attendance at the feast of dedication (John 10:22) — commemorating Antiochus's overthrow and the temple rededication — is merely a historical fact about what feast Jesus attended. It does not establish that the little horn prophecy refers to Antiochus.

The 2300 "evenings and mornings" cannot be literal 24-hour days. Applied literally they lead nowhere prophetically — they just stop. The year-day principle supported in Numbers 4, Ezekiel 4, and Numbers 14 must apply. Since the 70 weeks cut off from the 2300 days are themselves weeks of years and not literal weeks, the entire prophecy operates on the year-day scale.

Finally, the vision explicitly extends to "the time of the end" — a scope too broad to fit within a few centuries of the prophecy's writing. Antiochus Epiphanes was historically one of the weaker Seleucid rulers, not the super-powerful horn the prophecy describes. While Josephus interpreted the horn as Antiochus, 2,000 more years of theological study has refined understanding well beyond ancient sources.
