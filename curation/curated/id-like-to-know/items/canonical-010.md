## Entry `canonical-010a`

- `question`: `Why is the Holy Spirit sometimes referred to as "he" and other times as "it" in Scripture, as in Romans 8:26-27 and John 14-16?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Romans 8:26-27`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Romans 8:26-27 is read directly in the New King James Version. John 14-16 is named in the question but not opened. The "he/it" point is resolved by Greek grammar rather than by walking additional texts.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White, Acts of the Apostles, page 52; Bible Training School, March 1, 1906`
- `tags`: `Holy-Spirit, pneuma, Greek-grammar, neuter, personhood, Godhead, Trinity, Romans-8, Ellen-White, metaphors, oil-rain-fire-dove`

### Answer

The variation between "he" and "it" is a grammar issue, not a theology issue. In Greek, the word for spirit — pneuma — is grammatically neuter, and the article that goes with it is the neuter article. A literal translation can render that as "it." But when the context makes plain that the subject is the Holy Spirit and the Holy Spirit is a person, translators shift to "he" to reflect the referent rather than the grammatical gender. That is why the same translator can use both within a few verses. Nothing in the Greek requires reading the Holy Spirit as an impersonal force.

The personhood itself is settled elsewhere. The Holy Spirit is the third person of the Godhead. Ellen White wrote in Bible Training School, March 1, 1906, "There are three living persons of the heavenly trio; in the name of these three," naming Father, Son, and Holy Ghost — and that statement is from her directly, not a compilation. So three persons is the plain teaching, even where the Greek article happens to be neuter.

Beyond that, restraint is right. Ellen White also warns in Acts of the Apostles, page 52, that the nature of the Holy Spirit is a mystery men cannot explain because the Lord has not revealed it; bringing together passages to construct a human theory on something God has left unrevealed will not strengthen the church. Silence is golden on the how. It is also worth noting that the metaphors used for the Spirit — oil, rain, fire, dove, outpouring in the latter rain — describe function, not substance. The Spirit is not literally a liquid being poured in; those images picture what He does. And the Holy Spirit is not simply the divine nature of Christ transferred down to earth, because Christ's divinity and humanity are not separable in that way. The Holy Spirit is His own person.

## Entry `canonical-010b`

- `question`: `If heaven has no sin or sadness, how will we remember loved ones who did not make it?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Revelation 20:11-15; Revelation 21:1-8; Isaiah 65:17; Psalm 37:34`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Revelation 21:4 is read directly and its placement is argued carefully across Revelation 20-21. Isaiah 65:17 and Psalm 37:34 are read. The question's premise ("no sin in heaven") is corrected from Scripture and Ellen White — sin originated in heaven, and sadness is not sin.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White, Early Writings, page 17 (the "heaven is cheap enough" vision)`
- `tags`: `heaven, memory, lost-loved-ones, Revelation-21, wipe-away-tears, destruction-of-wicked, Isaiah-65, Psalm-37, sorrow, Ellen-White, Fitch, Stockman, final-judgment`

### Answer

The premise needs one correction before the answer. It is not quite right to say heaven has never had sin or sadness. Sin originated in heaven, in a perfect place. And sadness is not sin — the angels stopped their singing and laid down their harps during Christ's passion, and God Himself sorrows when people reject Him. What is true is that heaven now has no sin, because the devil has been cast out, and in the new earth there will be no more pain.

On the memory itself, start with the placement of Revelation 21:4. Revelation 20 ends with the wicked cast into the lake of fire. Revelation 21:1 is the climax of that scene: new heavens, new earth, the old passed away. Then verse 2 begins a new cycle that loops back before the destruction — the New Jerusalem descending after the millennium, before the wicked are destroyed. Inside that earlier window comes verse 4: "And God will wipe away every tear from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain." Every verb in the surrounding passage is future from that point, and death is still present because the wicked have not yet been destroyed. So the tears God wipes away are real tears, shed by the saints inside the city over those outside the city. There will be grief. God does not pretend otherwise.

After that, the memory is not preserved as ongoing torment. Isaiah 65:17 is plain: "Behold, I create new heavens and a new earth: and the former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind." Psalm 37:34 says that when the wicked are cut off, you shall see it — the saints will witness the judgment — but the carrying of that memory does not belong to them forever. That is where God's promise to wipe away the tear becomes an erasure, not a suppression. The pain of who is not there is borne eternally in the heart of God, not in ours.

Ellen White was shown this in vision. In Early Writings page 17 she was carried forward to the kingdom and met two of the 1844 pioneers who had died, Fitch and Stockman, who asked what the living had passed through. "We tried to call up our greatest trials, but they looked so small compared with a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory that surrounded us that we could not speak them out, and we all cried out, 'Hallelujah! heaven is cheap enough'; and we touched our glorious harps and made heaven's arches ring." Heaven will be so glorious that the memory of the former things will not rise to mind. The answer is not that we forget by effort but that glory and the presence of Christ displace the old.

## Entry `canonical-010c`

- `question`: `Is the Filioque how they supplant Jesus Christ with the pope?`
- `scripture_basis`: ``
- `source_scripture_usage`: `No Scripture is exposited. The answer is a short historical clarification; the speakers openly decline to endorse the premise that the Filioque supplants Christ with the pope.`
- `secondary_support`: ``
- `tags`: `Filioque, Nicene-Creed, Nicaea, East-West-schism, procession-of-the-Spirit, church-history, pope`

### Answer

The premise behind the question does not hold up, and the speakers say so directly. They do not see how the Filioque is connected to supplanting Christ with the pope.

What the Filioque actually is: the Latin word *filioque* means "and from the Son." The original creed said the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father. Later the Western church added "and the Son," so that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The Eastern church did not accept that addition and considered it a corruption of the doctrine of the Godhead. That dispute was one of the major factors in the schism between the Eastern and Western churches. It is a serious theological and ecclesial controversy in its own right, but it is not the mechanism by which the papacy substitutes itself for Christ.
