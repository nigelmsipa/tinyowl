## Entry `ilk-015a`

- `question`: `Why does Isaiah 65:20 seem to describe death and curse in the new earth if Revelation says those things will be gone?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Isaiah 65:17-25; Revelation 21:1-4; Revelation 22:3`
- `tags`: `Isaiah-65, new-earth, conditional-prophecy, Revelation-21, Revelation-22, classical-prophecy`

### Answer

Isaiah 65 is not a flat, one-layer prediction in which every detail moves directly into the final new earth without qualification.

Isaiah belongs to the class of prophecies first addressed to literal Israel under covenant conditions. In that setting, the promise looked toward the restored post-exilic community and described what life would have been if Israel had remained faithful to the covenant.

That is why Isaiah 65 can speak in the language of long life, blessing, and restored conditions, including statements that do not fit the final state in exactly the same way Revelation 21 and 22 do. Revelation describes the completed new earth where death, sorrow, and curse are gone. Isaiah 65 first speaks into a conditional restoration setting for Israel, and then echoes forward toward the end without carrying every local detail over in a wooden way.

So the Bible is not being treated as contradictory. Isaiah 65 gives covenant-restoration language that could have been fulfilled in relation to literal Israel. Revelation then gives the final and ultimate picture in which death and curse are abolished completely.

## Entry `ilk-015b`

- `question`: `How can Paul say in Romans 7 that with the mind he serves the law of God and with the flesh the law of sin? Does that mean a Christian serves both at once?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Romans 7:7-25; Romans 8:1-9; Matthew 6:24`
- `tags`: `Romans-7, Romans-8, law, flesh, spirit, sanctification, two-masters`

### Answer

No. You cannot serve God and sin as two equal masters at the same time.

Romans 7 is describing the struggle of a person who knows that the law is holy, just, and good, and who inwardly wants to do the will of God, yet finds the pull of the flesh still working against that desire. So when Paul says that with the mind he serves the law of God, the point is that his inner desire is on the side of God's law.

That does not mean the life of victory is found in living half for God and half for sin. Matthew 6 already rules that out: you cannot serve two masters. Romans 8 then provides the resolution. There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ and walk not after the flesh but after the Spirit. The flesh and the Spirit are opposed to each other, and the Christian life requires the flesh to be crucified rather than accommodated.

So Romans 7 describes the conflict, not a permanent arrangement in which sin and obedience share the throne together. Romans 8 then points to life in the Spirit, where there is victory and hope.

## Entry `ilk-015c`

- `question`: `Is the COVID-19 vaccine the mark of the beast?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Revelation 13; Revelation 14; Matthew 24:7`
- `tags`: `mark-of-the-beast, vaccine, COVID-19, Revelation-13, Revelation-14, worship, prophecy`

### Answer

No. The COVID-19 vaccine is not the mark of the beast.

Revelation 13 and 14 place the mark of the beast in the setting of false worship. The issue there is allegiance, worship, the beast, its image, and the enforcement of a counterfeit religious system. A vaccine is not a worship act, so it does not fit the prophetic category being described.

The mark is also connected with the image of the beast and its enforcement. If the image has not yet been formed, then the mark is not something to be identified in a medical product or public-health measure.

So do not let speculation distract you from the real issue. The mark of the beast is a worship issue in prophecy, not a virus, not a pestilence, and not a vaccine.
