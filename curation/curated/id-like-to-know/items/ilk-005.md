## Entry `ilk-005a`

- `question`: `What is the identity of the eighth in Revelation 17:11?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Revelation 17:8-11; Daniel 2; Daniel 7; Revelation 12; Revelation 13`
- `secondary_support`: `Maranatha, p. 187; J.N. Andrews, The Three Messages of Revelation 14, pp. 77-78; Signs of the Times, February 8, 1910`
- `tags`: `Revelation-17, eighth, seven-heads, papacy, united-states, persecuting-power, deadly-wound`

### Answer

The `eighth` of Revelation 17:11 is not an eighth head separate from the seven. The beast has `seven heads`, not eight. The `eighth` is the resurrected persecuting phase of the seventh head.

The argument begins with the structure of the passage itself. Revelation 17 repeatedly speaks of seven heads, so the number eight cannot mean that a brand-new head suddenly appears. Instead, the number is tied to the seventh head returning in a resurrected form. That is why the number eight is linked here to resurrection.

The next step is to identify what the heads represent. `Kings` and `kingdoms` are treated as interchangeable in prophetic language, as in Daniel 2 and Daniel 7. Mountains also point to kingdoms. So the seven heads are not seven individual popes, but seven successive powers.

Those persecuting powers are: Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, pagan Rome, papal Rome, the United States, and then papal Rome again in its restored persecuting phase. The United States, as the sixth kingdom, gives its power to the papacy and helps restore its persecuting authority. That restored phase is why the seventh is also called the eighth.

So the `eighth` is the papacy revived after the deadly wound, functioning again as a persecuting power through authority restored with the help of the United States.

## Entry `ilk-005b`

- `question`: `Will babies or little children who died before knowing good and evil be in heaven?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Deuteronomy 1:39; Matthew 19:14; Jeremiah 31:15-17`
- `secondary_support`: `Child Guidance, pp. 565-566; Selected Messages, book 3, p. 313; Heaven, p. 45`
- `tags`: `children, infants, abortion, salvation, heaven, accountability, parents, judgment`

### Answer

Scripture does not give a fully definitive answer for every case, so do not claim more certainty than God has given. God will do what is right in every case, and the judgment will make that righteousness plain.

Deuteronomy 1:39 is treated here as showing that little children can be described as having `no knowledge between good and evil.` That raises the question, but it does not by itself settle every case about children who died before moral accountability. So the answer moves carefully.

There is real comfort in the supporting material for faithful parents. Christ's words, `Suffer little children to come unto me, and forbid them not, for of such is the kingdom of God,` are applied alongside Jeremiah's promise that children will come again from the land of the enemy. The supporting statements then say that many little ones laid away before the time of trouble will be seen again, known again, and received in the heavenly courts.

The same supporting material also argues that the faithfulness of believing parents covers their children in a representative sense. In that line of reasoning, faithful parents may have strong comfort that children taken from them will be restored.

At the same time, the source refuses to make a universal ruling about every child of unbelieving parents. The cited statement is explicit: `we cannot tell, because God has not made known His purpose in regard to this matter.` So the right answer is not dogmatism, but reverence.

Even so, the source does affirm that some infants will certainly be there. *Heaven* describes little infants rising immortal and being received, including some whose mothers are not there. So you can say this much with confidence: some infants and little children who died will be in heaven, faithful parents have strong grounds for comfort, and the cases God has not explained should be left where He has left them.
