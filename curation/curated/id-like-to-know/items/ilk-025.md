## Entry `ilk-025a`

- `question`: `Did Israel break the Sabbath by marching around Jericho on the seventh day?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Joshua 6:2-16; Luke 4:16-19; Exodus 20:8-10; Matthew 12:12`
- `tags`: `Jericho, Sabbath, Joshua-6, holy-war, deliverance, Exodus-20, Luke-4`

### Answer

No. Israel did not break the Sabbath by marching around Jericho, because they were doing what God Himself commanded on that day.

Joshua 6 is plain. The Lord told Joshua to march around the city for six days and then seven times on the seventh day. God would not command an act of Sabbath-breaking as part of His own holy purpose.

That also shows something deeper about the Sabbath. The seventh day is not merely a day of inactivity. It is a day for God's work, God's victory, God's deliverance, and God's purpose. They were not doing their own work. They were doing the Lord's work.

That fits the wider Sabbath pattern. Jesus spoke on the Sabbath of liberty, healing, and deliverance, and He also taught that it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath. So Jericho does not weaken the Sabbath. It shows that the Sabbath is fully consistent with obedience to God's direct will.

## Entry `ilk-025b`

- `question`: `What does Paul mean when he says he delivered Hymenaeus and Alexander unto Satan?`
- `scripture_basis`: `1 Timothy 1:20; 1 Corinthians 5:4-5; 2 Timothy 4:14`
- `tags`: `Hymenaeus, Alexander, church-discipline, Satan, disfellowshipping, 1-Timothy-1, 1-Corinthians-5`

### Answer

Paul is speaking about putting people outside the fellowship and protection of the church so they will face the seriousness of their sin.

First Corinthians 5 helps explain the expression. There, delivering a man to Satan is tied to discipline meant to expose sin, break its power, and, if possible, bring the sinner to repentance so that he may finally be saved.

So one meaning is remedial: outside the church's fellowship, the person is forced to face what he has chosen, and that painful exposure may lead him back to God.

But there is also a more severe possibility in a case like 1 Timothy 1:20. If these men had hardened themselves beyond repentance, then the language may also reflect God leaving them to the path they stubbornly chose. Either way, Paul is not acting out personal revenge. He is recognizing that open rebellion cannot be sheltered inside the church as if nothing serious has happened.

## Entry `ilk-025c`

- `question`: `Could Christ have abandoned His mission and returned to heaven, or would that have been impossible because of prophecy and the Father's foreknowledge?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Matthew 26:39; Matthew 26:53; Matthew 5:18; James 1:13`
- `tags`: `Christ, free-will, foreknowledge, prophecy, Gethsemane, Matthew-26, Matthew-5, James-1, mission, covenant`

### Answer

Christ's struggle was real, and His obedience was real.

The Father knew from eternity what Christ would choose, but the Father's foreknowledge did not make the choice for Him. Knowledge is not compulsion.

That is why Gethsemane matters. Jesus truly felt the weight of the cup. He truly suffered in His humanity. He truly said that the Father could send more than twelve legions of angels (Matthew 26:53). Those were not empty words. They show that the trial was real.

And here is a detail that makes the struggle even more real: while Jesus was on this earth, He did not have access to the total plan. In heaven, He had seen the whole course of His ministry from beginning to end, but on earth He only knew day by day what the Father's will was for that day. So His obedience was walked out under genuine pressure, not performed with the full picture in front of Him.

But He did not abandon the mission. He remained faithful to the covenant, faithful to the Father's will, and faithful to the plan of redemption. If He had turned back, humanity would have perished, and not one jot or tittle of prophecy (Matthew 5:18) would have been fulfilled. But prophecy did not fail, because Christ freely obeyed all the way through.

So do not think of prophecy as a mechanism that erased Christ's moral choice. The Father knew what the Son would choose, but the Son still had to pass through the agony of choosing obedience under real pressure.
