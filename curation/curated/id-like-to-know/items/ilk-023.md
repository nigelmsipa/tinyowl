## Entry `ilk-023a`

- `question`: `What does it mean that Abel's blood cried from the ground and that, though dead, he still speaks?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 4:10; Hebrews 11:4; Leviticus 17:11; Matthew 23:34-35; Revelation 6:9-11`
- `tags`: `Abel, blood, state-of-the-dead, justice, Genesis-4, Hebrews-11, Revelation-6`

### Answer

It does not mean Abel was consciously alive somewhere speaking.

Genesis 4 and Hebrews 11 interpret each other. Abel is dead, yet he still speaks in the sense that his unjust death still testifies and still cries for justice before God. His spilled blood stands as witness.

Leviticus 17 says the life is in the blood, so the language is figurative. The point is not that blood has literal vocal cords. The point is that murder calls for divine reckoning.

Jesus uses the same kind of language in Matthew 23 when He speaks of the righteous blood shed on the earth, and Revelation 6 does the same when the slain are pictured as crying out for judgment. So read Abel's blood the same way: not as proof of conscious dead saints, but as a figure for a life violently taken and awaiting God's justice.

## Entry `ilk-023b`

- `question`: `When the Greek word "aion" is translated as "worlds", should it really be understood as "ages", and can those texts be used to prove there are inhabited worlds?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Matthew 13:49; Matthew 16:26; Matthew 24:21; Revelation 12:12`
- `tags`: `aion, ages, worlds, cosmos, translation, inhabited-worlds, Revelation-12, Matthew`

### Answer

In the passages discussed here, "aion" is better understood as "age" than as "world".

That is why the contrast is made with "cosmos", which is the ordinary word for the world. In texts like Matthew 13:49, "the end of the world" is really the end of the age. But in a text like Matthew 16:26, gaining "the whole world" is talking about the world itself, not an age.

So do not lean too heavily on `aion` texts as proof that there are other inhabited worlds. That is too thin a foundation for that claim.

If you want biblical evidence for other worlds, Revelation 12 gives a clearer line: `Rejoice, O heavens, and you that dwell in them.` That contrasts the dwellers in the heavens with the inhabitants of the earth. So yes, there is biblical evidence for other worlds, but these `aion` texts are not the strongest place to prove it.

## Entry `ilk-023c`

- `question`: `If spirit can mean breath, how can the Spirit lead us in Galatians 5, and does spirit always mean breath when it is not referring to the Holy Spirit?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 2:7; Galatians 5:18; Job 26:13; Job 33:4; Psalm 33:6; Psalm 104:29-30; Romans 8:2; Romans 8:11`
- `tags`: `spirit, breath, Holy-Spirit, Genesis-2, Galatians-5, creation, Romans-8`

### Answer

No. Spirit and breath are related ideas in Scripture, but they do not mean the exact same thing in every passage.

In Genesis 2, the breath of life is the life-giving power from God that animates the body. In passages about the Holy Spirit, such as Galatians 5, the reference is to the divine Person who leads, gives spiritual life, and recreates the heart.

The same Hebrew and Greek word family can carry both ideas. That is why Scripture can speak of God's breath in creation and also of the Spirit of God as the agent of creation. Job 33 says, `The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.` Psalm 104 also parallels breath and Spirit in giving and sustaining life.

So do not imagine some conscious ghost entering and leaving the body. In the physical sense, God's life-giving breath animates the creature. In the spiritual sense, the Holy Spirit gives new life, freedom, and moral renewal. One concerns physical life; the other also includes spiritual quickening.

## Entry `ilk-023d`

- `question`: `Was Daniel 2:44 fulfilled in a way contrary to Jewish expectations at Christ's first coming, and is the `glory of his reign` the same glory Paul describes in 2 Corinthians 3?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Daniel 2:44; 2 Corinthians 3:8-18`
- `secondary_support`: `The Desire of Ages, p. 34`
- `tags`: `Daniel-2, kingdom, Messiah, expectations, 2-Corinthians-3, glory, first-coming`

### Answer

Yes, Daniel 2:44 was bound up with expectations that were fulfilled contrary to the way many people expected.

Many were looking for the Messiah to establish an earthly kingdom that would visibly crush the kingdoms of this world. That is why so many were disappointed when Jesus did not come as a political king.

But 2 Corinthians 3 is speaking about something different. Paul is contrasting the glory of the old covenant administration with the surpassing glory of the new covenant seen in the face of Jesus Christ.

So do not merge those two ideas into one. The first concerns mistaken expectations about the visible form of Messiah's kingdom. The second concerns the covenant glory revealed in Christ and the righteousness of God shining in Him.
