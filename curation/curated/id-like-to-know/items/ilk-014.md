## Entry `ilk-014a`

- `question`: `Are the 24 elders in Revelation twenty-four literal representatives of only twenty-four unfallen worlds, or is the number symbolic?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Isaiah 24:21-23; Revelation 19; Revelation 20; 1 Chronicles 24; Luke 1:5-9; Colossians 1:16`
- `secondary_support`: `The Desire of Ages, closing pages; Review and Herald, September 25, 1900`
- `tags`: `24-elders, Revelation, unfallen-worlds, heavenly-council, sanctuary, symbolic-numbers, Isaiah-24`

### Answer

The elders are real representatives in God's heavenly council, but the number `24` does not mean that only twenty-four unfallen worlds exist.

Isaiah 24 is the main starting point. After the punishment connected with the second coming and the many days that follow, the chapter says that the Lord will reign `before His elders gloriously.` So God is shown there as having elders before Him in heaven.

The number `24` is then connected with sanctuary service. First Chronicles 24 divides the priestly service into twenty-four courses, and Luke 1 shows that arrangement still functioning in the days of Zacharias. So the number functions as a sanctuary-government number, a symbolic way of describing organized representation and administration before God.

That is why the number is not a census of the universe. The elders represent the worlds that never sinned, but `24` is symbolic, the way Revelation commonly uses numbers.

Colossians 1 also fits that larger picture by speaking of thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers. God's government is ordered, layered, and representative. So the twenty-four elders are not proof that there are only twenty-four worlds. They are representatives within God's organized heavenly administration.

## Entry `ilk-014b`

- `question`: `Why would William Miller be saved if he leaned to human wisdom instead of divine and did not fully accept further light?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Matthew 11:2-6; 1 Samuel 16:7`
- `secondary_support`: `Early Writings, p. 257; The Desire of Ages on John the Baptist in prison`
- `tags`: `William-Miller, accountability, judgment, John-the-Baptist, influence, heart, further-light`

### Answer

William Miller was not saved by his works. He is treated as a man judged mercifully and fairly by the God who knows the heart.

John the Baptist provides the biblical comparison. John announced the Messiah, yet while in prison he was pressed into doubt and sent word asking whether Jesus really was the Coming One. Jesus did not cast him off. He answered by pointing to the evidence and dealt with him in mercy.

That helps explain William Miller. He had carried a heavy burden for the Advent cause, had passed through repeated disappointment, was worn by labor and age, and was also influenced by the people around him. So his failure to receive further light is not treated as simple high-handed rebellion.

The underlying point is that God judges more deeply than human observers do. We see outward failure, but God sees the heart. He knows how much light a person grasped, how much pressure was brought to bear, and how much strength remained.

William Miller will be saved because Christ judged him righteously, not because Miller earned salvation. God used him greatly, bore with his weakness, and measured his case with a fairness that humans cannot duplicate.
