### Answer

Scripture does not directly classify mushrooms as clean or unclean, so do not speak dogmatically where neither the Bible nor Ellen White gives a direct ruling.

No biblical text and no Spirit of Prophecy statement dealing specifically with mushrooms is identified here. So mushrooms should not be turned into a test of faithfulness on the basis of texts that do not actually address them.

Caution and health judgment are still required. Some mushrooms are poisonous, and some are used in harmful or occult-associated ways. At the same time, mushrooms may also have nutritional value, and scientific claims about possible health benefits are noted.

So the answer is not `mushrooms are unclean.` The answer is that Scripture gives no direct prohibition, wisdom is required, and you should avoid turning a matter without explicit revelation into a rigid doctrinal rule.

## Entry `ilk-008b`

- `question`: `Does Psalm 104 teach that human beings should work only during the day and never at night?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 1:5; Psalm 104:19-23; John 3:1-2; Acts 20:7-11`
- `tags`: `night-work, labor, Psalm-104, creation-order, exceptions, procrastination`

### Answer

Psalm 104 teaches a general pattern, not an inflexible universal ban on night work.

The psalm describes the normal rhythm of creation: certain animals move at night, and man goes forth to his labor by day. That does show that daylight is the ordinary time for human labor and nighttime is ordinarily fitted for rest.

But that description is not absolute. Human necessity creates exceptions. Hospitals cannot close at night. Cities still need guarding. Emergencies still happen. Scripture itself shows godly activity at night: Nicodemus came to Jesus by night, Paul spoke late into the night, and Jesus sometimes spent the night in prayer.

So the answer is that day labor is the normal pattern, but Psalm 104 is not a law forbidding all night work in every circumstance.

## Entry `ilk-008c`

- `question`: `Were only Aaron's descendants allowed to be priests and high priests, and what was the role of the other Levites?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Numbers 3:45; Numbers 4; Hebrews 7:5, 11-14`
- `tags`: `Aaron, Levites, priests, high-priest, temple-service, tithe, Melchizedek`

### Answer

The tribe of Levi was set apart for the Lord's service, but the priesthood in the narrower sense belonged to Aaron and his descendants.

Numbers 3 presents the Levites as God's special possession in place of the firstborn of Israel. They were not simply idle temple residents. They had real duties: caring for the sanctuary, transporting its furnishings, teaching God's statutes, assisting in worship, and living from the tithe because they had no territorial inheritance like the other tribes.

Within that larger Levitical structure, Aaron's line held the priestly office in the stricter sense, including the high priesthood. So not every Levite was a priest, even though priests were Levites.

Hebrews 7 helps clarify the distinction. It speaks of the sons of Levi receiving the priesthood, but it also emphasizes that Jesus did not belong to Levi at all. He came from Judah, so His priesthood had to rest on a different order altogether: the order of Melchizedek.

So the answer is that the Levites had a broad range of sanctuary and teaching responsibilities, but the Aaronic line held the priestly and high-priestly office.

## Entry `ilk-008d`

- `question`: `If you sell a house and use the proceeds to buy another house, should you tithe any of that money?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 14:20; Malachi 3:8-10`
- `tags`: `tithe, increase, property-sale, house, offerings, stewardship, honesty`

### Answer

You tithe the `increase`, not the entire sale price.

The reasoning is straightforward. If the money originally invested in the house was already tithed when it first came to you as income, that principal is not being tithed again. But if the property increased in value, that gain is increase, and the tithe belongs on that increase.

So if you bought the house for one amount and sold it for more, the extra value is treated here as the part on which tithe should be returned. The source also stresses that tithe is not generosity but honesty. Offerings are where generosity enters, because offerings are given freely beyond what already belongs to God.

So the answer is yes: if the sale produced real increase, tithe should be returned on that increase, even if the rest of the money is being rolled into another house purchase.
