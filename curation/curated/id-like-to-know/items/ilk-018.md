## Entry `ilk-018a`

- `question`: `Is justification by faith the same as the new birth, and does Christ's imparted righteousness make obedience possible?`
- `scripture_basis`: `John 3`
- `secondary_support`: `Review and Herald, June 4, 1895; The Desire of Ages, p. 300`
- `tags`: `justification, new-birth, imputed-righteousness, imparted-righteousness, obedience, sanctification`

### Answer

Justification by faith is not something you should separate from the new birth and the sanctified life that follows it.

Christ's righteousness is imputed first. That means His righteousness is credited to your account, and you are accepted before God through Him. But the same Christ who justifies also gives new life. He does not declare you righteous while leaving you unchanged and unwilling to walk in obedience.

That is why the distinction between imputed and imparted righteousness must not be turned into a division. Imputed righteousness is the root. Imparted righteousness is the fruit. The title to heaven and the fitness for heaven are both found in Christ's righteousness.

So yes, Christ's imparted righteousness makes obedience possible, but only in the context of the new birth. You must be born again. Justification is not a permission slip to remain unchanged. It leads into a life where Christ's power is manifested in obedience.

## Entry `ilk-018b`

- `question`: `Why did Paul appeal to Caesar and go before Nero if Nero was such a wicked ruler?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Acts 25:7-12`
- `secondary_support`: `The Acts of the Apostles, pp. 430, 486-487; Christian tradition on Paul's later appearance before Nero`
- `tags`: `Paul, Caesar, Nero, Acts-25, appeal, providence, persecution, judgment`

### Answer

Paul appealed to Caesar because remaining under the reach of the Jerusalem authorities would have placed him in even greater danger and denied him justice.

Acts 25 shows the immediate situation. The Jews brought serious accusations they could not prove, yet Festus was willing to please them and proposed sending Paul to Jerusalem for trial. Paul understood what that meant. Jerusalem was not a neutral venue. It would have exposed him to those who wanted him dead.

So his appeal to Caesar was not a naïve trust in Nero's goodness. It was an appeal to the legal protection available to him as a Roman citizen. The supporting material says he knew he would be safer among the heathen than among those who had hardened themselves against the gospel.

And God overruled the situation. At Paul's first appearance before Nero, the charges were not sustained and he was released. That made the appeal wise in providence, even though Nero himself was a wicked man.

So do not measure the decision only by Nero's character. Measure it by the alternatives before Paul and by the providence of God, who used that appeal to preserve His servant longer and to place light before a ruler who otherwise could not say he had never received it.
