### Answer

This prophecy must be understood through biblical typology, where a historical "type" (a shadow or pattern) points to a future "antitype" (the perfect, ultimate reality). 

Verse 16 had a direct, historical fulfillment in Isaiah's day: within a couple of years, the kings of Syria and Samaria (Rezin and Pekah) were killed, bringing deliverance to Judah. However, the historical type does not perfectly encompass all the details of the prophecy. Verse 14 explicitly speaks of a virgin conceiving—a biological impossibility without divine intervention. This specific sign could only find its true, perfect fulfillment in Jesus Christ.

The Old Testament frequently uses such dual applications. For example, "Out of Egypt I called my son" initially referred to the nation of Israel, but the New Testament applies it perfectly to Christ. Likewise, while Isaiah 7 had a local context, the promise of a miraculously born "God with us" points ultimately to the Messiah taking on human flesh to become our perfect Savior.

## Entry `canonical-014b`

- `question`: `How do we interpret the institution of slavery in ancient Israel, especially the lifelong servitude of non-Israelites?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Leviticus 25:39-46; Deuteronomy 23:15-16; Exodus 1:11-14; Joshua 9`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Leviticus 25:39-46 is read extensively to show the rules governing Hebrew and foreign servants. Deuteronomy 23:15-16 is read to prove runaway slaves were not to be returned. Exodus 1:11-14 is read to define "rigor" using Israel's oppression in Egypt. Joshua 9 is summarized regarding the Gibeonites.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White (on God punishing America for the crime of slavery, and on the Gibeonites being grafted into Israel).`
- `tags`: `slavery, Old-Testament-law, Leviticus-25, Deuteronomy-23, Exodus-1, foreigners, indentured-servitude, human-dignity`

### Answer

Biblical slavery cannot be equated with Greco-Roman slavery or modern colonial slavery. Those later systems treated human beings as mere property to be exploited with "rigor"—the exact kind of abusive bondage Israel suffered in Egypt, which God condemned. In fact, God punished the United States for the high crime of slavery.

In ancient Israel, the system functioned more like indentured servitude or a social welfare system for debt relief. Even when foreigners became permanent servants (like the Gibeonites who tricked Joshua), they were not abused. They lived with their families, possessed land, and were grafted into Israel. 

God's laws heavily regulated the practice to protect human dignity. Slaves were explicitly granted Sabbath rest and could not be worked seven days a week. Furthermore, Deuteronomy 23 explicitly commanded that if a slave escaped from an abusive master, they were not to be returned—a law that completely contradicts modern fugitive slave laws. While the system allowed for foreign servitude, it strictly forbade cruelty, exploitation, and ruling over anyone with "rigor."
