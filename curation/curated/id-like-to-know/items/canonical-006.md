## Entry `canonical-006a`

- `question`: `Does 1 Corinthians 7:14 — that the children of a mixed marriage "would be unclean, but now they are holy" — mean children are born with a sinful nature?`
- `scripture_basis`: `1 Corinthians 7:13-14`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `1 Corinthians 7:13-14 is read directly on air. The answer stays inside that context and does not branch into broader sinful-nature theology.`
- `secondary_support`: ``
- `tags`: `1-Corinthians-7, mixed-marriage, unequally-yoked, children, sanctification, household, holy-influence`

### Answer

No, this is not a text about a child's nature at birth. It is about the home.

Paul is addressing a couple who were both unbelievers when they married and then one spouse came to faith. He is not addressing the question of whether to enter an unequally yoked marriage. His point is that the believing spouse should not automatically divorce. The believing spouse does not become defiled by the unbelieving one; the opposite moves in. The unbelieving spouse is sanctified by the believing one, and that holy influence spills over onto the children, so that they are called holy rather than unclean. If the marriage were torn up, that sanctifying influence on the home and on the children would be lost.

So "unclean" and "holy" here describe standing inside the covenant household under the influence of a believing parent, not the ontological condition of the child. The passage is not saying anything about sinful nature in either direction.

## Entry `canonical-006b`

- `question`: `How does the seventh commandment apply to angels and other non-human beings who do not marry? And why do animals procreate if they are not repopulating heaven?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 1:28; 2 Corinthians 6:14; Revelation 17`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Genesis 1:28 is read directly for the animal-procreation portion. Revelation's harlot imagery is summarized rather than read, and 2 Corinthians 6 ("be not unequally yoked") is paraphrased. No specific verse is opened for the seventh commandment itself.`
- `secondary_support`: ``
- `tags`: `ten-commandments, principles, adultery, spiritual-fornication, harlot, Lucifer, angels, Genesis-1, dominion, ecosystem, animals`

### Answer

The ten commandments are eternal in principle and adapted in expression. The fourth commandment, for example, mentions the manservant and maidservant — that is a human application of an eternal principle. The seventh commandment works the same way.

At the principle level, adultery and fornication are disloyalty. A pure woman in Scripture stands for a faithful church; a harlot stands for a church that has abandoned Christ and joined herself to the kings of the earth. Revelation's harlot committing fornication with the kings is that union of church and state — and it is a violation of the seventh commandment in principle. Paul uses the same logic when he tells believers not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers. Lucifer is the original fornicator in that sense: he forsook Christ and fell in love with himself, coveted a position that was not his, and in principle broke every one of the ten commandments. So the seventh commandment applies to every moral being in the universe. It governs loyalty, not only human sexuality.

On animals: they procreate because the ecosystem of this world requires it. God created animals for the life of the earth, foresaw sin and death entering, and gave them the capacity to replenish so that the order of nature would continue. Genesis 1:28, where God tells the humans to be fruitful and multiply and have dominion, sets up the human-animal relationship as stewardship, not exploitation. Animals multiply to serve nature's balance and to serve humanity's life on earth. That is the purpose; repopulating heaven is not.

## Entry `canonical-006c`

- `question`: `Can you show from the Bible only that the saints will hear the day and hour of Christ's coming?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Revelation 16:17-18; Revelation 14:13; Daniel 12:1-2`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Revelation 16:17-18 is read directly as the context of the voice from the throne saying "It is done." Revelation 14:13 and Daniel 12:1-2 are cited by reference. There is no single Bible text that states the day and the hour will be announced; the speakers acknowledge that openly and argue that the Ellen White scene fits inside the biblical sequence without contradicting it.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White, The Great Controversy, chapter "God's People Delivered"`
- `tags`: `day-and-hour, close-of-probation, time-of-trouble, seventh-plague, Revelation-16, it-is-done, special-resurrection, Revelation-14, Daniel-12, Sinai, Ellen-White`

### Answer

There is no single Bible verse that states, "God will announce the day and the hour." The speakers acknowledge that directly. What the Bible does give is the surrounding scene, and the announcement fits inside it.

This is after the close of probation, at the very end of the time of trouble, when every case is already decided. Revelation 16:17-18 places us there: the seventh angel pours out the last plague, a loud voice comes out of the temple from the throne saying, "It is done," and there are noises, thunderings, lightnings, and the greatest earthquake in human history. Whenever Scripture describes thundering, lightnings, and earthquake together, God is about to say something weighty — Sinai at the giving of the ten commandments is the pattern. So the moment in Revelation 16 is already a moment in which God speaks.

Inside that scene, the living righteous are glorified, and there is a special resurrection — those who pierced Him, and those who died in the faith of the third angel's message, which Revelation 14:13 marks with "blessed are those who die in the Lord from now on." Then Christ comes, and when He is above the earth the general resurrection of those who died before 1844 takes place, which Daniel 12:1-2 describes as the awakening of those who sleep in the dust.

So when Ellen White says in The Great Controversy that the voice from heaven announces the day and the hour, she is filling in a detail inside a sequence the Bible already gives. It does not contradict Christ's "no man knows the day or the hour," because that statement refers to our current knowledge before probation closes. After "It is done," the living righteous hear it because the decision has already been made and the deliverance is underway.

## Entry `canonical-006d`

- `question`: `Is it wrong to crochet on Sabbath while listening to Bibles or sermons to keep my hands busy, if it is not to sell?`
- `scripture_basis`: ``
- `source_scripture_usage`: `No Scripture is exposited here. The answer appeals to the general Sabbath principles of not buying, selling, or engaging in secular work, and then to conscience.`
- `secondary_support`: ``
- `tags`: `Sabbath, crochet, work, conscience, focus, principles-vs-lists, service, ADD`

### Answer

No moral issue here. Do not build lists of Sabbath rules that Scripture does not build. The biblical principles are plain — do not buy or sell, do not engage in secular work — and inside those principles God has given you a conscience. A sanctified conscience is the instrument that leads you to the right answer in your own case.

The question already carries its own answer. You are not doing this for gain; you are doing it because your hands need something to do in order for your mind to stay focused on the Word. That is not secular work; it is an aid to worship. If the crocheting were producing items to sell it would be a different question. If the items are a blessing to someone else — sweaters for the homeless or people in need — that is service, not labor.

Do not let others become your conscience. Ask the Lord to show you, keep the Sabbath holy from the heart, and do what actually helps you hear the Word.
