## Entry `ilk-011a`

- `question`: `Are there musical genres or styles, including certain uses of drums, that should not be used in church worship?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Daniel 3:1-7`
- `tags`: `music, worship, drums, genre, syncopation, church, emotions`

### Answer

The central issue is not the instrument by itself, but the way the instrument is used. A drum is not automatically evil, but music can be used in a way that stirs the lower nature, feeds physical excitement, and pulls the mind away from Christ-centered worship.

That is why this answer does not treat drums as a simple yes-or-no question. A drum can be used properly in ordered music, but many modern worship styles use rhythm in a way that is deliberately driving, sensual, and emotionally manipulative. In that sense, yes, some genres and styles should not be brought into church worship.

Daniel 3 is used as the biblical illustration. Nebuchadnezzar gathered people before the image and used music to prepare the senses for false worship. The instruments themselves were not the main problem. The way the music functioned within the worship setting was the issue.

So do not ask only, `Is this instrument present?` Ask what the music is doing. If it draws the mind to reverence, truth, and the message of God, that is one thing. If it bypasses judgment, excites the body, and turns worship into self-centered stimulation, it does not belong in the church.

## Entry `ilk-011b`

- `question`: `What place do cultural music styles have in Christian worship?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Daniel 3:1-7`
- `secondary_support`: `Music: Its Role, Qualities, and Influence`
- `tags`: `music, culture, worship, principles, sacred-music, church`

### Answer

Culture is not automatically bad, and culture is not automatically good. The real test is whether a musical style follows the principles of good worship music.

There is room for genuine cultural variety. Different peoples use different instruments, tonal colors, and musical idioms, and some of those can be used beautifully in Christian worship. But cultural location does not sanctify a style that works against reverence, clarity, and spiritual edification.

The concern here is that music can overshadow the message. Worship is not supposed to become an hour of emotional buildup with the preaching reduced to an afterthought. The message should remain central, and the music should serve that message rather than dominate it.

So the right balance is not to reject culture as such. Use cultural forms that harmonize with biblical principles of worship, and refuse forms that condition the church to crave excitement, sensuality, or entertainment more than truth.

## Entry `ilk-011c`

- `question`: `What do the references to tambourine and dance in Psalms mean, and do they justify dancing or casual secular music in worship?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Psalm 149:3; Psalm 150:4; 2 Samuel 6:14-16`
- `secondary_support`: `Patriarchs and Prophets`
- `tags`: `dance, tambourine, psalms, David, worship, music, church`

### Answer

The references to tambourine and dance do not justify importing modern sensual or romantic dancing into worship.

Biblical dance in these texts is chiefly an expression of joy in specific historical and festive settings, not a standing pattern for sanctuary worship. David's dancing before the ark is the key example, and it is understood as a unique outburst of rejoicing, not a model for the kinds of dancing commonly defended today.

That is why this source pushes back against using David to justify a man and woman dancing closely together or using bodily excitement as a worship method. The cited supporting material also distinguishes David's act from modern dancing that leads toward licentiousness and sensuality.

Tambourines fall under the same principle. Their presence in a text does not settle every question by itself. What matters is whether the use of the instrument and the total form of the worship are reverent, spiritually sound, and in harmony with holy worship rather than fleshly excitement.

## Entry `ilk-011d`

- `question`: `Is penal substitution biblical?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Galatians 3:13; 2 Corinthians 5:21`
- `tags`: `atonement, penal-substitution, cross, curse, substitution, gospel`

### Answer

Yes. If penal substitution means that Christ bore the penalty of sin in the sinner's place, that is biblical.

The biblical language of curse and substitution points in that direction. Christ was made a curse for His people so that blessing could come to them, and He who knew no sin was made to be sin for us. That does not mean Christ became sinful in character. It means He took the sinner's place under judgment.

So the biblical doctrine does include penal substitution in that sense: Christ bore what the sinner deserved so that the sinner might receive what belongs to Him.

## Entry `ilk-011e`

- `question`: `What is the recommended diet for Seventh-day Adventists awaiting the final crisis and translation?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Genesis 1:29; Daniel 1`
- `tags`: `diet, health, Adventist, Genesis-1, Daniel-1, vegetarian, translation`

### Answer

The ideal diet is the original diet God gave: plant-based food drawn from what He first appointed for human nourishment.

Genesis 1:29 is treated here as the basic statement of that ideal, and Daniel's experience is used as a confirming example. So the direction of the answer is toward fruits, grains, nuts, and the produce God originally gave, rather than toward flesh foods as a norm.

At the same time, real-world conditions still matter. People live in different circumstances, and some regions do not offer the same abundance of food choices. So practicality and context still matter. But where the ideal is reasonably accessible, the counsel is to move as closely as possible to the diet God originally established.

So the recommendation is not an artificial extremism. It is a return, as far as possible, to the original divine pattern.

## Entry `ilk-011f`

- `question`: `Should Seventh-day Adventists vote and participate in political affairs?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Daniel 6; Genesis 41`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen White counsel on voting and religious liberty`
- `tags`: `voting, politics, Adventist, religious-liberty, civic-duty, Joseph, Daniel`

### Answer

Yes, Adventists may vote, but they should do so carefully, conscientiously, and without partisan frenzy.

The emphasis here is not on political agitation or party zeal. It is on moral responsibility. Research the candidates, consider whether they support principles such as religious liberty, and do not place into office those whose actions would make you morally complicit in evil.

Participation does not mean immersion in party spectacle. Adventists are not called to turn themselves into campaign activists driven by the spirit of political rivalry. At the same time, voting itself is not treated as forbidden. Joseph and Daniel are even mentioned as biblical examples of God's servants occupying public positions.

It is not erroneous for an Adventist to vote. In the right circumstances, it may be a duty of responsible citizenship. But it should be done with principle, restraint, and careful attention to issues such as religious freedom.
