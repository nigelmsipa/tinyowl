## Entry `ilk-007a`

- `question`: `Why does Judges 19-20 describe such brutal abuse, and what is the purpose of that story?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Judges 19-20; Jeremiah 17:9; Romans 3:10-18; Isaiah 49:14-15`
- `tags`: `Judges-19, Judges-20, brutality, women, sin, human-heart, abuse, Old-Testament`

### Answer

Judges 19-20 is there to show what sin looks like when it is not restrained by the Spirit of God. Scripture does not hide the ugliness of evil or soften what the human heart becomes when it is surrendered to wickedness.

That is why the story is so disturbing. The point is not that God delights in brutality. The point is that men, even men who outwardly belong to the covenant community, can become shockingly cruel when sin rules the heart. Romans 3 describes that condition plainly: no righteousness, no fear of God, feet swift to shed blood, and destruction in their ways.

So the purpose of the story is revelatory and cautionary. It exposes the depth of human corruption. It shows what happens when people follow the dictates of an unconverted heart rather than the Lord. The same principle explains brutality more broadly, whether men against women or men against men. Sin dehumanizes.

At the same time, the story should not be read as a revelation of God's character. Isaiah 49 is brought in to remember that God's heart is compassion, not cruelty. These narratives are in Scripture to tell the truth about sin, warn you where rebellion leads, and drive you to the only real remedy: repentance and the restoring grace of God.

## Entry `ilk-007b`

- `question`: `Where do the children in Isaiah 11:8 come from if the scene is after the millennium and there is no marrying in heaven?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Isaiah 11:6-9; Matthew 22:30`
- `tags`: `Isaiah-11, children, new-earth, millennium, restored-creation, heaven, speculation`

### Answer

Do not force a speculative answer where Scripture has not given one. Isaiah 11 is chiefly promising the restoration and harmony of God's kingdom, not explaining every biological detail of life on the renewed earth.

The passage presents a world put right: peace among creatures, safety where danger once ruled, and a created order healed from the curse. That is the main burden of the prophecy. So the child in Isaiah 11 should first be read as part of that picture of total restoration.

Do not press earthly categories too mechanically into the world to come. Matthew 22 says the future order will not simply mirror present social arrangements. That means some questions about how every future detail works may remain veiled until God reveals more.

A second line of reasoning is that Isaiah is also speaking into the history of Israel. What literal Israel failed to realize because of unbelief will be fulfilled in God's final kingdom with spiritual Israel. So the prophecy does not need to be reduced to a narrow puzzle about post-millennial childbearing.

The safe conclusion is this: Isaiah 11 assures you that God's restored kingdom will include the full reality of peace, safety, and joy that sin destroyed. Where Scripture has not explained the mechanics in detail, leave the unrevealed details with God.

## Entry `ilk-007c`

- `question`: `How can Revelation 19 speak of fowls eating the flesh of kings if the destruction at Christ's coming leaves Satan and his angels to contemplate the results of sin?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Revelation 18:2; Revelation 19:17-21; Psalm 74:18-19; Psalm 7:1-2; Psalm 22:12-13; 1 Corinthians 15:32`
- `secondary_support`: `Sketches from the Life of Paul, p. 78`
- `tags`: `Revelation-19, birds, beasts, symbolism, demons, second-coming, judgment, Babylon`

### Answer

Revelation 19 is using symbolic language. The fowls are not meant to be read as literal scavenger birds solving a logistical problem after the second coming. They represent demonic powers, and the wild beasts represent wicked human enemies.

Revelation 18:2 provides the key. Babylon is described as the dwelling place of demons, the prison of every foul spirit, and the cage of every unclean and hateful bird. That identifies the birds with demonic agencies. The Psalms likewise use beasts symbolically for hostile human powers, and 1 Corinthians 15:32 is explained in the same way when Paul speaks of fighting beasts at Ephesus.

So Revelation 19 is not a crude literal scene. It is a symbolic portrayal of real destruction. Christ comes, the wicked are overthrown, and the demonic powers that drove the rebellion are shown for what they are. The sword from Christ's mouth is His word, not a literal blade, and the feast imagery dramatizes the total defeat of the rebellious powers.

That destruction is also connected with Satan's work of stirring the wicked to ruin and even to mutual destruction. The larger point is that sin, under demonic control, ends in chaos, violence, and judgment.
