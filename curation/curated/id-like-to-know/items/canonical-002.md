### Answer

1 Timothy 5:22 sits inside Paul's sustained instructions to Timothy on managing elders. From verse 17 onward, Paul addresses how elders are honored, how accusations against them are handled, and how they are to be rebuked when guilty. Verse 21 commands Timothy to observe these things "without prejudice, doing nothing by partiality." Then verse 22: "Do not lay hands on anyone hastily, nor share in other people's sins. Keep yourself pure."

The laying on of hands is ordination. The context has not shifted — Paul is still talking about elders. If Timothy commissions a man who is unqualified or morally compromised, he becomes a partner in whatever damage that man will do to the church. That shared guilt is what "share in other people's sins" means here.

Why does haste create shared guilt? Because qualification is a matter of proven life, not stated intention. 1 Timothy 3:2-7 lists what a bishop must be: blameless, the husband of one wife, temperate, sober-minded, hospitable, able to teach, not given to wine, not violent, not greedy, ruling his own house well. Titus 1:6-9 repeats the same logic for elders. These are observable patterns across time — you cannot evaluate them in a week or a season.

Romans 1:32 closes the argument from the other direction: those who "not only do the same but also approve of those who practice them" are judged alongside the practitioners. Appointment is a form of approval. Hasty appointment is approval without evidence — and it carries the same weight before God.

### Answer

Proverbs 25:21-22 establishes the instruction before naming its effect: "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat; and if he is thirsty, give him water to drink: for so you will heap coals of fire upon his head, and the Lord will reward you." The sequence matters — give first, coals follow. The coals are not the motive; they are the consequence of genuine, unguarded kindness.

1 Samuel 24 shows what this looks like before Proverbs gives it a name. Saul was hunting David with three thousand men. When Saul entered the very cave where David was hiding, David had every opportunity to end the threat. Instead he cut only the edge of Saul's robe — and even that troubled his conscience (verse 5). He let Saul leave unharmed, then called out and showed him what mercy had just been extended. Saul's response in verses 16-17: he wept, and said, "You are more righteous than I, for you have rewarded me good, whereas I have rewarded you evil." The coals fell — not as punishment but as conviction. The kindness broke something in him.

Paul draws on the same proverb in Romans 12:19-20 and frames it explicitly as the alternative to revenge. Verse 19 forbids personal vengeance: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, says the Lord." Paul then quotes Proverbs word for word and calls it the active response: feed the enemy, give him drink, heap coals on his head. The logic is clean — step out of the way, do good, let God settle the account. The coals are not your weapon. They are what happens when you refuse to be one.
