- `question`: `What does 1 Timothy 5:22 mean — "Do not lay hands on anyone hastily, nor share in other people's sins. Keep yourself pure"?`

### Answer

To share in other people's sins means to bear the institutional guilt of what you officially endorsed — not the casual guilt of proximity, but the active guilt of appointment. The laying on of hands in verse 22 is ordination, and Paul's logic is precise: if you commission an unqualified man and he damages the church, you own a portion of that damage. Verse 21 commands impartiality. Then verse 22 names the mechanism of partiality: haste.

**Scripture**

1 Timothy 3:1-7 and Titus 1:5-9 explain why haste is structurally dangerous. Qualification for eldership is a matter of proven, observable life: blameless, the husband of one wife, temperate, sober-minded, hospitable, able to teach, not given to wine, not violent, ruling his own house well, having a good testimony from outsiders. These things cannot be known in a season. They require time and watching. To commission without that evidence is to approve without basis — and Romans 1:32 makes the principle explicit: those who "approve of those who practice" wrong are judged alongside the practitioners. Appointment is approval. Hasty appointment is approval without evidence.

**Spirit of Prophecy**

Ellen White applies this principle directly to Adventist church organization. In *Testimonies to the Church*, vol. 5, she warns against advancing men to office before their character and fitness have been demonstrated through sustained faithfulness. Men should not be thrust into positions of responsibility simply because they are available, articulate, or popular. The church suffers long-term damage when unproven men occupy offices that require trustworthiness built over time — and those who placed them there share the accountability.

**Historical / Scholarly**

The apostolic church was careful about this from the beginning. In Acts 6, the first appointment of deacons involved the whole community selecting men who were "of good reputation, full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom" — observable, community-confirmed character, not individual preference. The laying on of hands in that context (Acts 6:6) ratified what was already evident to the congregation. The same pattern runs through the appointment of elders in Acts 14:23 — Paul and Barnabas appointing elders in every church after a period of observation. The early church documents (the *Didache*, the *Apostolic Tradition* of Hippolytus) extended examination periods before ordination precisely because the apostolic churches understood that unqualified leadership corrupts a community from within, and those who commissioned them cannot escape responsibility.

**Pastoral**

The passage is asking a harder question than it first appears. It is not merely warning against bad judgment in choosing leaders. It is warning that institutional decisions carry moral weight. Timothy cannot ordain and then step back from what that man does. The question "Did I share in his sins?" is answered not by intention but by process: Did you take the time to know him? Did you examine the qualifications? Did you ask the people who live alongside him? If not — if you moved fast because someone was available, or because saying no felt awkward — then the answer is yes.

## Entry `canonical-002b`

- `scripture_basis`: `Proverbs 25:21-22; 1 Samuel 24:5, 16-17; Romans 12:19-20; Micah 6:8`

### Answer

The coals are not the goal. They are what happens when the kindness is real.

The proverb commands action first — feed your enemy, give him water — and names the effect second. The burning is not the strategy. It is the consequence of doing right without calculating what it will produce.

**Scripture**

1 Samuel 24 is the narrative demonstration of the proverb before the proverb was written. Saul had three thousand men hunting David. When Saul entered the cave where David was hiding, David had every opportunity to end it. He cut only the hem of Saul's robe — and even that troubled him (verse 5). He let Saul walk out, then called after him and showed him the evidence of mercy just extended. Saul's response in verses 16-17: he wept. "You are more righteous than I, for you have rewarded me with good whereas I have rewarded you with evil." The coals fell — not as punishment but as conviction. The act of unguarded kindness broke something in him that three thousand soldiers and a manhunt could not reach.

Paul quotes the same proverb directly in Romans 12:19-20, and he frames it as the active alternative to revenge. Verse 19 removes the option of personal vengeance entirely: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay, says the Lord." Then the instruction: feed, give drink, heap coals. The logic is clean — step out of the way, do good, let God handle the settling. The coals are not your weapon. They are what happens when you refuse to become one.

**Spirit of Prophecy**

Ellen White in *Thoughts from the Mount of Blessing* treats this principle as the practical outworking of the Sermon on the Mount. To love the enemy and do good to those who hate is not sentimentality — it is a display of a character transformed by grace, which carries convicting power that argument and force cannot. She describes how the silent testimony of a gracious life reaches people that no sermon can. The burning coals, in her reading of the spirit of the principle, are the shame and conviction produced in the antagonist when they encounter goodness where they expected retaliation.

**Historical / Scholarly**

The "coals of fire" idiom has a proposed background in ancient Egyptian culture: a penitent would carry a dish of burning coals on the head as a visible sign of shame and remorse. If that background is in view, Proverbs is saying that genuine kindness to an enemy produces in them the very posture of repentance — publicly visible contrition — that no amount of pressure could force. The idiom is disputed, but what is not disputed is the consistent Hebrew moral logic: the reversal of expected behavior produces the deepest moral disruption. David's mercy to Saul is the clearest illustration in the canon. Paul saw it the same way — which is why he reached for this verse from Proverbs when teaching Roman believers how to deal with their persecutors.

**Pastoral**

The speakers make one qualification that should not be lost: the "coals = Holy Spirit conviction" interpretation — while popular — goes beyond what the text says. The text says you will heap coals on his head. It does not say the Holy Spirit will. The distinction matters because the popular reading can subtly reintroduce the revenge motive through the back door ("I'll be kind to him so the Spirit can convict him") — which is not the instruction. The instruction is simpler and harder: give him what he needs. What happens after that is not your assignment.
