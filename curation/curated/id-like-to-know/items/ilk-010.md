## Entry `ilk-010a`

- `question`: `Why would angels use a golden card to enter and leave heaven?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Malachi 3:16; Psalm 56:8; Daniel 7:9-10; Revelation 20:12`
- `secondary_support`: `Early Writings, p. 39; Christian Experience and Teachings of Ellen G. White, p. 101`
- `tags`: `angels, heaven, records, books, order, harmony, golden-card`

### Answer

Do not build a doctrine on the golden card itself. The main point is that heaven operates with perfect order and harmony, and the language of records fits the broader biblical picture of books and testimony in heaven.

The source notes that Ellen White mentions the golden card only briefly and does not explain it in detail. So the answer stays modest. Daniel 7, Revelation 20, Malachi 3, and Psalm 56 all show that heaven keeps records. Not because God forgets, but because His government is transparent before the watching universe.

Within that framework, the golden card is understood here as possibly connected to angelic commission and report. Angels are sent on missions, angels return, and heaven is a realm of ordered administration. So the card may reflect authorization, assignment, reporting, or witness. But that remains an inference, not an explicit revealed explanation.

So the safe answer is this: the golden card signals order in heaven and fits the biblical theme of heavenly records, but Scripture and the cited passages do not explain enough for dogmatic speculation beyond that.

## Entry `ilk-010b`

- `question`: `How can the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 66-70 be connected with Daniel 9 if the seventy weeks end in AD 34?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Daniel 9:24-27; Matthew 23:34-39; Matthew 24:15; Luke 21:20`
- `secondary_support`: `The Great Controversy, pp. 27-28`
- `tags`: `Daniel-9, seventy-weeks, Jerusalem, AD-70, abomination-of-desolation, covenant, judgment`

### Answer

The destruction of Jerusalem does not fall inside the seventy-week chronology itself, but it does stand as the consequence of what happened during that period.

Daniel 9 gives the seventy weeks for the Jewish people and the holy city. Within that period, Messiah appears, confirms the covenant, and is cut off in the midst of the final week. The chronology reaches its close in `AD 34`. But Daniel 9:26-27 also points beyond the internal timetable to the later destruction of the city and sanctuary.

That is why the passage must be read carefully. The destruction is mentioned as a result of covenant rejection, not as a dated event inside the seventy-week clock. Matthew 24 confirms this by using the language of the `abomination of desolation`, and Luke 21 identifies that desolation with Jerusalem surrounded by armies.

The source also stresses that judgment on Jerusalem was delayed in mercy. Christ's denunciation in Matthew 23 is followed by continued appeals through the apostles. So the fall of Jerusalem comes later, after extended longsuffering, as the historical outworking of Israel's corporate rejection of Messiah.

So the answer is that `AD 66-70` is related to Daniel 9 by consequence, not by chronology. It lies outside the seventy weeks themselves, but it fulfills the desolation those verses warn about.

## Entry `ilk-010c`

- `question`: `What does the uncovering of feet mean in Ruth 3?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Ruth 3:1-13`
- `tags`: `Ruth, Boaz, uncovering-feet, marriage, kinsman-redeemer, custom, modesty`

### Answer

In Ruth 3, uncovering Boaz's feet is a culturally intelligible, symbolic act connected with Ruth's appeal for redemption and marriage. It is not presented here as sexual immorality.

Naomi sends Ruth to Boaz as a near kinsman. Ruth uncovers his feet, lies down there, and when Boaz wakes, she asks him to take her under his wing because he is a close relative. That gesture is understood here as a modest but clear appeal: receive me, cover me, and fulfill the duty of a kinsman-redeemer.

The strongest internal evidence is Boaz's own response. He calls Ruth a `virtuous woman` and treats her request as honorable, not scandalous. The entire exchange assumes moral integrity on both sides. So the act must be read through the cultural setting of the time, not through modern suspicion.

So the uncovering of feet means Ruth was making a symbolic and lawful appeal for Boaz to marry and redeem her. It does not justify treating the scene as premarital sexual license, and the source explicitly rejects using the passage that way.
