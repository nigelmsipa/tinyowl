## Entry `canonical-020a`

- `question`: `If the sun stood still for a whole day in Joshua 10:13, would the weekly cycle have been thrown off, making the next Sabbath fall a day late?`
- `scripture_basis`: `Joshua 10:12-14`
- `source_scripture_usage`: `Joshua 10:12-14 is read directly by Salazar for context. The argument then shifts entirely to Ellen White and logical reasoning about the definition of a day. No further Scripture is opened.`
- `secondary_support`: `Ellen G. White, Testimonies to Ministers, page 135; Signs of the Times, March 20, 1879; Patriarchs and Prophets, page 509`
- `tags`: `Joshua-10, sun-stood-still, Sabbath, weekly-cycle, creation-week, miracle, day-definition, Ellen-White, Testimonies-to-Ministers, Signs-of-the-Times, Patriarchs-and-Prophets`

### Answer

The weekly cycle has not been broken since creation week. That is not negotiable. The question is how to account for Joshua's extra day without disrupting the count — and the answer lies in how God defines a day.

Ellen White in Testimonies to Ministers page 135 writes that God means "the day of 24 hours which he has marked off by the rising and setting of the sun." On the day of Joshua's battle the sun did not go down and the moon did not move. No sunrise opened it; no sunset closed it. By God's own definition, that interval was not a day. It falls outside the week's count the same way a fraction of time that has no rising and setting simply does not register as a day. The weekly cycle was not interrupted; it ran continuously alongside a period that did not qualify as a day at all.

Ellen White in Signs of the Times, March 20, 1879, confirms the continuity: "The first week in which God performed the work of creation... was just like every other week. The great God in his days of creation and day of rest measured off the first cycle as a sample for successive weeks till the close of time. The weekly cycle of seven literal days... has been preserved and brought down through Bible history." She does not name Joshua as an exception. The cycle held.

Ellen White adds in Patriarchs and Prophets page 509 that this miracle "testifies that the creation is under the control of the creator" — it was a demonstration of divine authority, not a disruption of divine order.

A practical point on doubt: if Sunday-keepers maintain that the day they keep is the same day Christ rose — trusting the continuity of the weekly cycle across the centuries — then the Sabbath must be equally intact. You cannot hold the resurrection-day continuous and the creation-Sabbath scrambled by the same count.

### Answer

The two texts the questioner cites do not prove consubstantiation, and neither does the rest of Scripture.

1 Corinthians 10:16 asks, "Is it not the communion of the blood of Christ... the communion of the body of Christ?" The word is *communion* — participation, memorial identification — not literalized flesh and blood. 1 Corinthians 11:27-29 warns against eating "in an unworthy manner" and "not discerning the Lord's body." The unworthiness in context is about self-examination, unconfessed sin, and failing to grasp what the symbols represent — not about failing to chew literal flesh.

John 6 is the decisive passage. When Jesus said "unless you eat my flesh and drink my blood, you have no life in you," many disciples heard it literally and were offended. He clarified in verse 63: "It is the spirit who gives life; the flesh profits nothing. The words that I speak to you are spirit and they are life." Physical eating is explicitly not what he meant. He means receiving him through his word — assimilating his character and life through Scripture. John 6:66 records that those who refused this interpretation and took it literally walked away and never returned. The literalist reading produced apostasy.

Jesus himself named the Supper a memorial: "Do this in remembrance of me." That is his own definition of the act. Nothing in 1 Corinthians overrides it.

Hebrews 10:10 closes the argument: "By that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all." Christ was offered once. Both transubstantiation and consubstantiation implicitly re-present that sacrifice at every table. The typology of Moses and the rock makes the point concrete: Moses was told to speak to the rock the second time but struck it instead — and Ellen White says he ruined the type, because the rock (representing Christ) was to fall under the rod of judgment only once. The once-for-all sacrifice of Calvary cannot and need not be repeated.
