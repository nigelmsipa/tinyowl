### Answer

Deuteronomy 14:26 is not a license to drink intoxicants. Read in context, it is part of the second-tithe instruction for pilgrimage feasts — a provision for long-distance travel where the tithe in kind could not be transported. The text says "whatever your heart desires" because it is describing how to convert grain and livestock into something portable. It is not issuing a blanket endorsement of any substance that produces the heart's desire.

**Scripture**

The principle the speakers apply is straightforward: God does not contradict himself. If Deuteronomy 14:26 granted license for alcohol, it would stand in direct contradiction to the rest of Scripture's witness. Proverbs 20:1 — "Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise." Habakkuk 2:15 pronounces a woe on anyone who puts the bottle to a neighbor's lips. The Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:3) treats abstinence from wine and strong drink as a mark of consecration — which implies that intoxication is the opposite of a consecrated state, not a neutral act.

Christ did not contradict his own teaching. The unfermented wine he provided at Cana was described as wholesome and refreshing — "its effect was to bring the taste into harmony with a healthful appetite." If he had sanctioned fermented alcohol, his teaching against it and the entire biblical temperance witness would collapse. God's word does not give with one hand what it takes away with the other.

The hermeneutical principle at work is: when a text appears to permit something the broader canon consistently warns against, the answer is to read the text more carefully, not to pit it against the canon. The Deuteronomy text is explaining a practical provision for a specific occasion. It is not a theological statement about the morality of alcohol consumption.

**Spirit of Prophecy**

Ellen White, in the *Review and Herald*, December 29, 1910, supplies the deeper principle: "It is the little foxes that spoil the vines. The little neglects, the little deficiencies, the little dishonesties, the little departures from principle that blind the soul and separate it from God... It is the little things of life that develop the spirit and determine the character." The point as applied here: a willingness to drink "just a little" because of an apparent textual loophole is exactly the kind of small departure that shapes character in the wrong direction.

**Historical / Scholarly**

The Hebrew words in view are *yayin* (wine) and *shekar* (strong drink or similar drink). Both appear throughout the Old Testament in positive and negative contexts — wine gladdens the heart (Psalm 104:15) but also destroys it (Hosea 4:11). The existence of both registers in the same vocabulary indicates that the same word could describe a range of beverages from grape juice to fermented wine depending on context. What determines the evaluation is not the substance per se but whether its use aligns with the overall moral and health framework of the covenant.

In ancient Near Eastern pilgrimage culture, tithe feasts were celebratory communal meals. The second tithe in Deuteronomy was specifically for the worshiper's household at the sanctuary. The instruction to buy "whatever your heart desires" reflects the feast's joy, not a nutritional or moral endorsement of every possible beverage. The feast was in God's presence — a context that, throughout Scripture, is associated with holiness, not license.

**Pastoral**

The speakers make the principle vivid: nobody drives at the edge of a cliff on purpose. The instinct to ask "how close can I get to the line?" is itself a symptom of a misdirected heart. Big decisions — whether someone ultimately drinks or stays sober, whether someone slides into compromise or holds a clear line — are the accumulation of small decisions made before anyone is watching.

Elisha began his ministry carrying water for Elijah. He did not start out as a great prophet; he was faithful in a small, invisible role. The reason Daniel and his friends could stand firm before Nebuchadnezzar's image — willing to die rather than worship it — was that they had spent years guarding small things: what they ate, what they drank, what they allowed into their minds. Clear minds enable prophetic faithfulness. The man who has trained himself to say "it probably doesn't matter" in small things has already begun to lose the capacity to say "it absolutely matters" in large ones.
