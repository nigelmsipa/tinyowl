## Why this matters

This testimony is worth engaging seriously for two reasons.

First, it is representative. The arc — deeply sincere faith → independent Bible reading + political disillusionment + evidential questions → deconversion — describes a significant and growing category of person. The objections she raises are not unusual; they are the standard cluster that shows up in post-evangelical deconversion accounts.

Second, the SDA perspective actually answers several of her core objections differently from the evangelical Christianity she was raised in — and better. She was taught eternal torment in hell. She was not taught the state of the dead. She was taught the flood but apparently not flood geology. She encountered hypocritical politics-religion fusion that SDA prophetic theology specifically predicts and warns against. Her journey was partly a fight against doctrines SDAs also reject. That is worth noting.

## Questions extracted from the testimony

### Cluster A — Historical and evidential

**aa-001** — Is the claim that "500 witnesses saw the risen Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:6) reliable, or does it ultimately rest on a single source?

**aa-002** — Is there geological or archaeological evidence for a worldwide flood as described in Genesis 6–9? What is the SDA response to the claim that no such evidence exists?

**aa-003** — When claimed facts taught in Christian contexts turn out to be wrong or overstated, does that invalidate the theological claims built on them? How do you distinguish between the reliability of Scripture and the reliability of things Christians say about Scripture?

### Cluster B — Doctrinal questions she was fighting against

**aa-004** — Is eternal torment in hell the correct biblical teaching about the fate of the lost? (She describes being afraid of "eternal torment in hell" — this is precisely what SDA anthropology and the state-of-the-dead doctrine corrects.)

**aa-005** — If someone was taught a false doctrine (e.g., eternal torment, hellfire) and that doctrine contributed to their deconversion, does their rejection of the false doctrine count as a rejection of Christianity — or a rejection of a corruption of it?

### Cluster C — Faith, evidence, and the nature of belief

**aa-006** — Is it rational to believe in God without empirical proof? What constitutes a "good reason to believe"? Where does faith differ from gullibility, and where does scepticism differ from honest inquiry?

**aa-007** — Can someone genuinely, wholeheartedly believe and still lose their faith? Does losing faith prove the faith was never real? (She addresses this directly — "they say you didn't really believe, but I really, really believed.")

**aa-008** — If someone prays sincerely and persistently and receives no perceivable response, what does that say about God's existence or responsiveness? What is the theology of unanswered prayer?

### Cluster D — The church, hypocrisy, and politics

**aa-009** — What should a Christian do when church leaders they trusted begin endorsing political figures or ideologies that appear to contradict the values they preached? Does leadership failure disprove the faith?

**aa-010** — Is the fusion of Christianity with political power and nationalism a prophetically expected development, and what does Scripture say about it? (From the SDA angle: Revelation 13, the image of the beast, religious nationalism — this is exactly what the SDA prophetic framework predicts.)

### Cluster E — Morality and meaning without God

**aa-011** — Can morality exist without God? She claims her moral standards improved after leaving Christianity. How do you respond without dismissing her experience?

**aa-012** — Is the Christian claim that atheists suppress the truth and are secretly aware of God consistent with testimonies like this one — where a person describes genuinely and desperately wanting to hold on to faith?

### Cluster F — Religious trauma

**aa-013** — Is "religious trauma" a legitimate category? She describes OCD, depression, and anxiety that she now attributes to religious upbringing. How should Christians respond to this framing? Where does genuine spiritual formation end and psychological harm begin?

## Research prompts

### Cluster A — Historical/evidential

**Prompt for aa-001 (500 witnesses):**
> The claim that over 500 people witnessed the risen Christ (1 Corinthians 15:6) is sometimes challenged on the grounds that Paul is the only source for this number and the 500 are nowhere else attested. Address this:
> 1. What is Paul actually saying in 1 Corinthians 15:1-8, and how does the structure of that passage bear on its reliability?
> 2. How early is this tradition? (The consensus dating of the 1 Corinthians 15 creed to within a few years of the crucifixion.)
> 3. Is "only one source" a valid objection, and why or why not?
> 4. What does the word "most of whom are still living" (v.6) suggest about Paul's intent and the verifiability of the claim?

**Prompt for aa-002 (Noah's Flood / geological evidence):**
> Address the claim that there is no evidence for a worldwide global flood and that the ark has never been found:
> 1. What does the Genesis 6–9 text actually claim, and what would physical evidence of such an event look like?
> 2. What is the SDA/creationist response to flood geology? (Key works: George McCready Price, Leonard Brand, Ariel Roth's *Origins: Linking Science and Scripture*)
> 3. What are the strongest actual geological evidences cited by flood geologists?
> 4. What is the state of the Ark search evidence honestly — what has been claimed, what has been verified, and what remains uncertain?
> 5. If the Ark is never found, does that falsify the flood narrative?

**Prompt for aa-003 (overstated Christian claims):**
> When Christians overstate evidence (e.g., claiming something is proven that isn't), and a sincere believer discovers the overstatement, how should this be handled theologically?
> 1. Distinguish between the reliability of Scripture and the reliability of popular Christian apologetics claims.
> 2. What is the difference between "we don't have evidence for X" and "we have evidence against X"?
> 3. Is honest uncertainty compatible with faith?

### Cluster G — Jesus-specific objections

**Source:** *5 Things About Jesus That Aren't So Great (including his supposed "sacrifice")*
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ziSORH5GTOA
Speaker: Promise Backland

**aa-014** — The sacrifice wasn't impressive. Humans sacrifice their lives for each other all the time — and they do it permanently. Jesus was only dead for about a day and a half before supposedly resurrecting. He didn't even have to offer the kind of permanent sacrifice a human would. Others have been through much worse torture, pain, and death than Jesus ever was.

**aa-015** — For someone operating on behalf of an all-knowing, all-loving God, Jesus was a poor communicator. He spoke in parables and contradictions — was he pro-violence (bringing a sword, buy a sword) or pro-peace (turn the other cheek, love your enemies)? He said faith the size of a mustard seed could move mountains — but nobody has ever moved a mountain by believing it. Was that a metaphor? Why didn't he say so, knowing people would go to war over his words?

**aa-016** — An all-knowing God should have condemned slavery. Instead, Jesus came to *fulfill* the laws that regulated slave ownership and slave-beating. He used slaves routinely in his parables. He never once said "do not own other humans as slaves." His words were subsequently used in writings to justify slavery.

**aa-017** — Jesus made cult-like demands of his followers: hate your family, let the dead bury their dead. The practical result is people who have disowned family members for faith — caring more about dogmatic belief than living, breathing human beings right in front of them. These are not the words of a God who values community and family.

**aa-018** — The Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12 — "do to others what you would want them to do to you") is oversimplified and not that wise. People are different. Treating others how *you* want to be treated ignores what *they* actually want. A better rule: treat others how *they* want to be treated. An all-knowing God should have known this.

**aa-019** — Jesus only had about three years of recorded ministry. For someone who could supposedly do anything, he didn't do much. If you could heal children from terminal illness, you'd heal every child you could. Jesus performed some small, selective miracles and left. Other philosophers and religious figures offer more interesting and wiser teaching. Without the supernatural claims being true, he's mildly interesting at best.

**Prompt for aa-014 (the sacrifice wasn't impressive / only a day and a half):**
> She makes two distinct sub-arguments: (a) the willingness to die is not unique — many humans would die for all of humanity if their death would guarantee the result, and (b) Jesus's death wasn't even permanent — he was back after ~36 hours. Address both:
>
> 1. **On willingness**: She's right that humans can be willing to die for others. The question is not willingness but *sufficiency*. A human death pays one human's debt. The wages of sin is *death* — but not just biological death. What makes Christ's death uniquely sufficient? The identity of the victim (Creator bearing the penalty owed by all creation), and the nature of what was experienced: Matthew 27:46 — the cry of dereliction is the second death, complete severance from the Father, not merely biological death. That is not on offer from a human volunteer.
>
> 2. **On "only a day and a half"**: Is duration the measure of sacrifice? A soldier who dies and is later revived still died. The atonement was completed on the cross — "It is finished" (John 19:30). The resurrection does not undo or diminish the sacrifice; it vindicates it. The debt was paid. The raising is the receipt.
>
> 3. **On "others have been through worse torture"**: The physical suffering of crucifixion, while severe, was not what made the death unique. Many have been crucified. The weight of bearing the sin of all humanity, and the separation from God that entails, is what was unique — and that is not visible from the outside. Address this directly: she's right that the physical suffering alone is not uniquely impressive.
>
> 4. The harder honest question: why does an all-powerful God need the mechanism of substitutionary sacrifice at all? Address the logic of the atonement, not just the facts of it.

**Prompt for aa-015 (bad communicator / parables / contradictions):**
> She names three specific objections: parables cause confusion, sword vs. turn-the-other-cheek, and mustard-seed-moving-mountains (why didn't he say it was a metaphor?). Address each:
>
> 1. **On parables as poor communication**: Jesus explains the design in Matthew 13:10-17. He is not hiding truth from honest seekers — he is filtering. Those who genuinely want to understand ask and receive explanation (verse 11). The parables have survived 2,000 years precisely because they are stories, not propositions. Assess her claim against the actual evidence of how his teachings traveled.
>
> 2. **On sword vs. turn the other cheek**: Matthew 10:34 ("I came not to bring peace but a sword") — read verses 35-37 immediately following: the "sword" is explicitly family division over truth. It is not a call to violence. "Turn the other cheek" (Matthew 5:39) is about personal dignity and refusing retaliation — it operates at a different register from self-defense or national policy. These are not contradictions; they are instructions for different situations. What would a genuine contradiction look like?
>
> 3. **On mustard seed / moving mountains**: She makes a fair point: if it's metaphor, the text doesn't announce it. Address honestly. Matthew 17:20 — the mustard seed saying is followed by a specific demonstration (the disciples couldn't cast out a demon; Jesus did). The "mountain" is likely an idiomatic expression for a large obstacle. But she's right that metaphor in a context that presents itself as literal instruction creates interpretive work. How should this be handled honestly rather than defensively?
>
> 4. Her strongest point: an all-knowing communicator who knew people would go to war over his words might have been clearer. Acknowledge what is real in this objection. What is the theological response to the problem of interpretive diversity?

**Prompt for aa-016 (Jesus and slavery):**
> She makes a specific claim: Jesus said he came to fulfill, not abolish, laws that regulated slave-beating (Matthew 5:17 + Exodus 21). He used slaves in parables. He never explicitly condemned slavery. His words were used to justify slavery. Address each:
>
> 1. **Matthew 5:17 and the fulfillment of the law**: What does "fulfill" mean in context? Matthew 5:17-20 is about the moral law, not about endorsing every civil ordinance in Mosaic legislation. The Sermon on the Mount that follows doesn't regulate slave-beating — it radicalises the ethic of human dignity.
>
> 2. **Exodus 21 and slave regulation**: The Mosaic regulations on slavery constrained the worst practices of the ancient world rather than endorsing them wholesale. A slave who was killed was to be avenged (v.20). A slave permanently injured was to be freed (v.26-27). These are protections embedded in a context where slavery already existed — not a divine endorsement of the institution.
>
> 3. **The trajectory argument**: She is asking for an explicit proof text condemning slavery. That text doesn't exist. But the trajectory of the gospel dismantled the institution over time: Galatians 3:28, Philemon, the dignity principles of the Sermon on the Mount. The abolition movement was substantially driven by Christians who took the trajectory seriously. This is the honest response: no explicit condemnation, but a moral logic that ultimately made the institution unsustainable.
>
> 4. **The hardest version of her objection**: If Jesus was all-knowing and knew his words would be used to justify centuries of chattel slavery, why didn't he say it explicitly? Address this directly. The absence is real. What do you do with it?

**Prompt for aa-017 (cult-like demands):**
> She focuses on the practical outcome: people have actually disowned family members for faith. Address:
>
> 1. **"Hate your family" (Luke 14:26)**: The parallel in Matthew 10:37 reads "loves father or mother more than me." In Hebrew/Aramaic comparative idiom, "hate" in this construction means "love comparatively less." The same Jesus commands honouring parents (Matthew 15:4), provides for his mother from the cross (John 19:26-27), and raises a widow's only son (Luke 7:11-15). The demand is for priority ordering, not actual hatred.
>
> 2. **"Let the dead bury their dead" (Luke 9:60)**: This is a hard saying. Acknowledge it. The most defensible reading: let the spiritually dead attend to the things of the spiritually dead — this person doesn't need you for this; the call is urgent. But it is a demanding statement and should not be softened into something comfortable.
>
> 3. **The practical outcome she names — family disownment**: She is right that this has happened. Is that the fault of the teaching or the application? What does the teaching actually produce in someone who follows it as intended versus someone who uses it as a club?
>
> 4. **On the "cult" characterisation**: Cults demand isolation, control information, create financial and social dependency, and prohibit exit. Jesus sent people back to their homes (Mark 5:19). He allowed followers to leave (John 6:67). He had no institutional infrastructure to benefit from. What is the meaningful distinction between "cult-like" and "cult"?

**Prompt for aa-018 (Golden Rule oversimplified):**
> She makes a specific, concrete argument: her birthday-celebration example. You go all out for her birthday because that's what you'd want — but she finds it uncomfortable. A better rule is "treat others how *they* want to be treated." Address:
>
> 1. Her proposed rule is actually *downstream* of the Golden Rule. The Golden Rule doesn't say "assume others have your preferences" — it says extend the same moral seriousness and care to others that you want extended to yourself. Asking "what do they want?" *is* treating them how you'd want to be treated: with attentiveness to your actual preferences.
>
> 2. Matthew 7:12 in context: Jesus frames it as the summation of the Law and the Prophets. It is a principle of moral orientation, not a social protocol that replaces judgment. It generates the specific rules; it is not itself the specific rule.
>
> 3. Her proposed improvement ("treat others how they want to be treated") is actually harder to operationalize: it requires omniscience or constant inquiry. The Golden Rule is a starting orientation; it expects you to refine it with knowledge of the other person. That is not a flaw — that is how principles work.
>
> 4. The deeper challenge in her argument: if morality is subjective (as atheism tends toward), on what basis is *her* improved Golden Rule binding? She's appealing to a moral standard while critiquing the foundation that grounds one.

**Prompt for aa-019 (limited ministry / selective miracles / just another philosopher):**
> She argues: three years of ministry, selective small-scale miracles, other philosophers and religious figures are equally or more interesting. If he could heal all children from terminal illness, why didn't he? Address:
>
> 1. **On the scope of miracles**: The Gospels don't present a selective miracle account — they present representative accounts. John 21:25 explicitly notes that not everything Jesus did was recorded. The claim that he "didn't do much" is an argument from the silences of a selective historical record.
>
> 2. **Why didn't he heal every sick child?**: This is the problem of selective divine intervention — one of the hardest questions in theology. Address it honestly: this question is not closed by clever argument. It requires engaging the broader question of why God allows suffering at all.
>
> 3. **Compared to other philosophers**: She says other figures offer more interesting and wiser teaching. Name who she might mean and engage the comparison honestly. What does Jesus say that Buddha, Confucius, or Socrates doesn't? The resurrection claim is the differentiator — not merely the quality of the ethical teaching. If the supernatural claims are false, she is right that he's one interesting teacher among several. If they're true, the comparison category collapses.
>
> 4. **"I created Jesus in my mind"**: She makes a genuinely important point at the end: as a Christian she had projected her own image of "perfect" onto Jesus. This is a real phenomenon. What is the difference between the constructed Jesus of religious upbringing and the Jesus of the primary documents read critically?

## Notes on the SDA advantage in this conversation

Several of her objections hit against evangelical Christianity specifically, not against Scripture:

| Her objection | Evangelical position she rejected | SDA position |
|---|---|---|
| Eternal torment in hell | Eternal conscious torment | Annihilationism / conditional immortality |
| No flood evidence | Young-earth with weak defenses | Young-earth with flood geology tradition |
| Church endorsing political power | Widespread in US evangelicalism | Prophetically predicted, warned against |
| 500 witnesses / Bible claims overstated | Popular apologetics overstates | Same honest apologetics needed |
| Moral improvement after leaving | "You must be immoral to leave" | Love and moral growth are compatible with following truth wherever it leads |

This does not mean the SDA answers are easy or that they close every question she raises. But it does mean the conversation is more nuanced than "Christianity vs. atheism" — it is partly "one form of Christianity vs. Scripture."
